Why is it useful to distinguish a fault from a failure?	A fault is an underlying defect or disruption, while a failure is the user-visible inability to meet the system's specification, so not every fault has to become a failure.
Why are hardware faults unavoidable in distributed systems?	Physical components and entire facilities eventually fail, so the system must assume disks, memory, power, networks, and even data centers can disappear.
Why do mundane software mistakes often cause more catastrophic outages than hardware faults?	Incorrect error handling can turn a recoverable condition into process termination, ignored corruption, or other user-visible failures.
Why is configuration management a major resiliency concern rather than mere housekeeping?	Configuration changes can break systems just like code changes, but often with less scrutiny and with effects that appear only later when the value is finally used.
Why can configuration faults be especially hard to catch early?	A bad setting may not be exercised until hours or days after it was changed, so the causal link is delayed unless validation happens at change time.
Why should configuration changes be versioned, tested, and rolled out like code?	They can create outages just as easily as software changes, so they need the same discipline around review, validation, and release safety.
Why are humans often dangerous single points of failure?	Manual operational sequences depend on one person executing many steps correctly under pressure, while automation performs repetitive procedures more consistently.
Why should every system component be examined by asking what happens if it fails?	That is the simplest way to uncover single points of failure and decide whether to add redundancy or at least reduce the component's blast radius.
Why are slow network calls often more dangerous than obviously failed ones?	They create gray failures where callers keep waiting, timing out late, or retrying unpredictably, which degrades the system before anyone can clearly identify a hard failure.
Why are resource leaks such a common cause of gray failures?	They gradually consume finite resources like memory, threads, or sockets, making a process slower and less useful long before it finally crashes.
Why can missing timeouts turn resource pools into hidden failure amplifiers?	A blocked call may never return its thread or socket to the pool, so enough stuck operations can silently exhaust the pool and stall unrelated work.
Why is sudden load pressure more dangerous than a steady increase in traffic?	Gradual growth can often be met by scaling capacity, but abrupt surges from seasonality, expensive requests, or attacks can overwhelm the system before it adapts.
Why can a small initial fault trigger a cascading failure?	When components depend on each other, one failure shifts load or errors onto others, increasing their chance of failing until the problem spreads through the system.
Why can retries make a partially degraded system collapse faster?	If timeouts trigger more requests against already strained dependencies, the recovery mechanism adds load precisely where capacity is already lowest.
Why are metastable failures hard to recover from even after the original fault is gone?	Feedback loops can keep bouncing overload around the system, so the application remains stuck in a degraded mode until a strong corrective action breaks the cycle.
Why should resiliency work be prioritized by risk instead of by trying to eliminate every possible fault?	The best use of effort is to address failures with the highest combination of likelihood and user impact rather than spending equally on unlikely low-damage scenarios.
