Why can a server become unavailable under extreme load before the operating system's connection queue is full?	It often exhausts internal resources like memory, threads, sockets, or files first, so response times collapse before the port-level queue becomes the main limit.
Why is load shedding better than trying to accept every request when a server is already at capacity?	Rejecting excess work quickly preserves resources for requests already in flight instead of letting overload slow down everything until the whole service becomes unusable.
Why can load shedding usefully prefer rejecting some requests over others?	If requests differ in priority or remaining value, dropping low-priority or already-aging work protects the requests most worth finishing.
Why can't load shedding fully protect a service if load keeps rising?	The server still pays some cost to accept enough of each request to reject it, so eventually even rejection overhead can dominate and degrade the system.
Why does load leveling help when clients do not need an immediate response?	A message channel decouples incoming load from service capacity, letting the consumer process requests at its own pace instead of being hit directly by spikes.
Why is load leveling good for short spikes but not a complete answer to sustained overload?	A queue can smooth temporary bursts, but if arrivals keep exceeding completions, backlog growth eventually becomes its own failure mode.
Why are load shedding or load leveling often paired with autoscaling?	These patterns protect a service from immediate overload, but only added capacity can sustainably absorb a higher long-term request rate.
Why is rate limiting different from load shedding even though both reject requests?	Load shedding reacts to the local stress of one process, while rate limiting enforces quotas based on broader usage state such as requests per API key across the system.
Why is `429 Too Many Requests` more useful than a generic failure when throttling clients?	It tells well-behaved clients that the request failed because a quota was exhausted, so they can back off instead of treating it like an arbitrary server error.
Why is rate limiting useful even when clients are not malicious?	It protects the service from buggy or overly aggressive clients that would otherwise monopolize resources by accident.
Why is rate limiting also a pricing mechanism, not just a resiliency one?	Quotas can align resource usage with paid tiers, so heavier users fund the additional capacity they consume.
Why is rate limiting only a partial defense against DDoS attacks?	Attackers can keep sending requests after being throttled, and the service still pays some cost to accept enough traffic to identify and reject it.
Why does distributed rate limiting require coordination?	The quota must reflect total usage across multiple service instances, so no one process can enforce it correctly from purely local state.
Why is bucketing requests into time intervals more practical than storing every request timestamp for rate limiting?	Buckets compress many requests into a few counters, making memory usage depend on window granularity rather than on total request count.
Why does a sliding-window rate limiter need weighted overlap with multiple buckets?	The active time window may span parts of adjacent buckets, so approximating current usage requires combining them proportionally instead of reading just one counter.
Why are asynchronous batched updates to the shared rate-limit store often a good trade-off?	They reduce load on the shared store and avoid a transaction per request, at the cost of some temporary inaccuracy in quota enforcement.
Why can serving from the last known rate-limit state be safer than failing closed when the quota store is unavailable?	Rejecting all requests because the limiter's backing store is down can hurt the business more than temporarily enforcing quotas approximately.
Why does constant work improve reliability under stress?	If the system performs roughly the same amount of work regardless of how many changes or requests arrive, overload is less likely to trigger surprise slow paths or unstable behavior.
Why can doing more predictable work be better than doing only the minimum necessary work?	Constant work may waste some capacity, but it often buys simpler, more testable, and more self-healing behavior than complex incremental logic.
Why does preallocating bounded capacity fit naturally with constant-work and cellular designs?	A fixed upper bound lets the system be stress-tested at its real worst case and scaled by adding new cells instead of discovering new failure modes at ever-larger sizes.
