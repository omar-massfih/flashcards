Why are transactions valuable in distributed systems?	They let developers reason as if a group of operations succeeds atomically or not at all, hiding many failure-interleaving edge cases behind a stronger abstraction.
Why are distributed transactions much harder than transactions inside one database?	They must coordinate atomic commit and isolation across multiple independent processes and storage systems that can fail separately.
Why is atomicity so important in workflows like money transfer?	Without it, one side effect can succeed while the matching compensating side effect fails, leaving the system in an externally visible inconsistent state.
Why is the "C" in ACID less useful than the other letters when reasoning about transaction mechanics?	It refers to application-defined invariants rather than a specific systems mechanism, so it tells you less about how the database actually behaves.
Why is serializability the strongest practically useful isolation level to aim for by default?	It guarantees concurrent transactions have the same effect as some serial execution, eliminating a broad class of race conditions without forcing developers to reason about interleavings.
Why is strict serializability stronger than plain serializability?	It adds a real-time guarantee, so once a transaction completes its effects must be visible to later transactions rather than merely appearing in some abstract serial order.
Why is stronger isolation usually slower?	Preventing more anomalies requires more coordination, waiting, aborts, or bookkeeping, all of which reduce concurrency.
Why do pessimistic concurrency-control protocols use locks?	They block conflicting transactions before harm occurs, which avoids wasted work in conflict-heavy workloads at the cost of waiting and deadlock handling.
Why can two-phase locking guarantee serializability?	If transactions acquire locks before release and hold them until completion, conflicting operations cannot interleave in ways that violate a serial order.
Why do deadlocks arise naturally in lock-based concurrency control?	Transactions can wait on each other's locks in a cycle, so progress stops unless the system detects the cycle and aborts someone.
Why does optimistic concurrency control work well when conflicts are rare?	It lets transactions run without blocking and pays the coordination cost only at validation time, which is efficient when restarts are uncommon.
Why is optimistic concurrency control a poor fit for conflict-heavy workloads?	Many transactions do work that is later discarded at validation time, wasting effort compared with blocking earlier.
Why is MVCC so attractive for read-heavy workloads?	It lets read-only transactions see a stable snapshot without blocking writers or being aborted by concurrent updates.
Why doesn't MVCC eliminate the need for concurrency control on writes?	Writes can still conflict with each other, so the system still needs a protocol such as locking or optimistic validation to serialize them.
Why is compare-and-swap with version numbers such a useful practical pattern?	It gives a lightweight form of optimistic concurrency control for one object, allowing a process to detect interference without implementing full transactions.
Why does write-ahead logging help guarantee atomicity and durability?	By recording the intended changes before applying them, the system has enough information to redo committed work or undo partial work after a crash.
Why is two-phase commit the classic protocol for distributed atomic commit?	It lets all participants first promise they can commit and only then makes a single all-or-nothing decision for the whole transaction.
Why is two-phase commit considered blocking?	Once a participant says it is prepared, it may be unable to safely proceed until the coordinator later tells it to commit or abort.
Why can replicating the coordinator and participants make two-phase commit more robust without making it simple?	Replication reduces single-node failure risk, but the protocol still requires expensive coordination and remains harder than ordinary consensus.
Why is Spanner notable in the transactions landscape?	It combines replication, 2PL, 2PC, MVCC, and carefully bounded clocks to offer globally distributed strict-serializable transactions.
