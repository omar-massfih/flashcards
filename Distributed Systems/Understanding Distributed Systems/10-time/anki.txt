Why is ordering events much harder in a distributed system than in a single-threaded process?	Different processes run concurrently and do not share a perfectly trusted global clock, so there is no single timeline that all nodes can use to agree on event order.
Why are physical wall clocks a poor foundation for ordering events across nodes?	Their readings can drift apart and be adjusted by synchronization, so timestamps from different machines can disagree or even appear to move backward.
Why can't NTP give distributed systems a perfectly reliable global notion of time?	It must estimate clock skew across a network with unpredictable latency, so synchronization is always approximate rather than exact.
Why is a monotonic clock better than a wall clock for measuring elapsed time on one node?	A monotonic clock never jumps backward or forward due to clock corrections, so duration measurements remain stable even when wall time is adjusted.
Why is a monotonic clock not enough for ordering events across different nodes?	It measures elapsed time relative to a local arbitrary starting point, so timestamps from different nodes are not directly comparable.
Why do logical clocks use causality rather than physical time to order events?	Causal relationships are more reliable than synchronized timestamps because message sending and receiving establish observable ordering constraints even when clocks disagree.
Why does sending a message create a synchronization point between processes?	Any operation performed before the send must have happened before any operation that depends on receiving that message.
Why are Lamport clocks useful even though they do not track real time?	They ensure that if one event happened-before another, the first event gets a smaller logical timestamp, which is enough for many ordering problems.
Why can't Lamport timestamps alone prove that one event caused another?	Timestamp order is only a one-way guarantee: causality implies order, but order does not necessarily imply causality for unrelated concurrent events.
Why are vector clocks stronger than Lamport clocks for reasoning about distributed events?	They can distinguish causal order from true concurrency, so when one vector timestamp is less than another, the happened-before relationship is guaranteed.
Why are vector clocks harder to scale than simpler logical clocks?	Each timestamp must track one counter per process, so storage and message overhead grow with the number of participants.
