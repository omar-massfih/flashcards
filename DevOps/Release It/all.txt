Why is "feature complete" not the same as "production ready"?	Feature completion proves the system does something; production readiness proves it can keep doing it under real load, failure, and operational constraints.
Why is passing QA a poor proxy for production readiness?	QA shows that known scenarios work; production tests whether the system survives unknown traffic patterns, hostile inputs, and long-running operational stress.
Why should software design focus on what a system must not do, not only what it should do?	Users and businesses suffer more from crashes, data loss, security failures, and outages than from missing a small feature, so failure behavior is part of the design.
Why is "design for production" analogous to "design for manufacturability"?	Both treat downstream operation as a first-class design constraint so the product can be delivered and run at low cost and high quality.
Why can optimizing for development speed alone be financially irrational?	A one-time shortcut in development often creates recurring operational costs, and long-lived systems spend far more time being operated than being built.
Why can investments in safer deployment mechanisms have outsized ROI?	They trade a recurring outage tax for a one-time engineering cost; for example, spending once on zero-downtime releases can prevent years of repeated release downtime.
Why do early architectural decisions have outsized long-term effects?	Early boundaries and decompositions harden into team structure, funding, and delivery processes, making them expensive to reverse once the organization forms around them.
Why does getting software into production early help architecture?	Production is where real constraints finally appear, so early release turns unknowns into feedback before bad assumptions become entrenched.
Why does pragmatic architecture focus on change instead of an ideal end state?	Systems live under shifting load, business needs, and failure modes, so architecture should make improvement, deployment, and measurement easier over time.
Why is software's real value delivered only in production?	Everything before production is preparation; the system helps users and creates business value only when it runs successfully in the real world.
Why should restoring service take precedence over root-cause investigation during an outage?	Every extra minute of downtime increases damage, so incident response should first shrink user impact and investigate deeply only after service is back.
Why are prebuilt diagnostic scripts so valuable during incidents?	They capture the evidence needed for postmortems without forcing responders to improvise or prolong the outage.
Why is the most recent change often a good starting hypothesis in a postmortem?	Recent changes frequently alter the system state that triggered the failure, so they provide a practical place to begin even though they are not proof of cause.
Why can status-page monitoring falsely suggest that a dependency is healthy?	A shallow health check may exercise only a lightweight path while the real request path is deadlocked, exhausted, or otherwise unusable.
Why are remote calls without effective timeouts dangerous?	A downstream failure can pin caller threads indefinitely, turning one sick dependency into a system-wide hang.
Why can cleanup code be the true source of a production outage?	If cleanup throws before later cleanup steps run, it can silently leak scarce resources until the whole system blocks.
Why can a failover expose bugs that were dormant before the failover?	Long-lived pooled resources may look valid until the topology changes; for example, a database connection pool can hold stale connections that fail only in edge-case cleanup paths after a primary failover.
Why can one small resource-handling bug cripple many upstream systems at once?	When many consumers share a dependency, exhausting that dependency's pool turns a local bug into a broad outage through contention and blocking.
Why is it unrealistic to rely on code review or testing to eliminate every production bug?	Some failures appear only under rare timing, topology, or load conditions, so resilient systems must assume that some bugs will escape.
Why is containing failure propagation more important than trying to write perfect software?	Interdependent systems inevitably contain defects, so survival depends on preventing one component's bug from taking down everything connected to it.
